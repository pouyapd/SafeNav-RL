\title{Deadlocks of Learned Navigation Policies Behind a Control-Barrier-Function Safety Filter: Mechanisms and Planning-Based Recovery}
\author{Pouya Bathaei Pourmand\\[2pt]\small \authorcheck{affiliation, postal address, e-mail and ORCID}}
\date{17 November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A control-barrier-function (CBF) safety filter can keep a learned navigation policy collision-free, but the robot may then stall before reaching its goal. We study such deadlocks for a simulated unicycle whose speed is limited by a one-step discrete-time CBF filter. Two filter properties frame the analysis: its one-step guarantee does not depend on the turn rate, and near an obstacle the headings that permit motion shrink to a half-plane. Classifying the timeouts of ten proximal policy optimisation agents, in scenarios that a geometric oracle confirmed solvable, reveals two mechanisms whose balance depends on training. Agents trained without the filter are held at the boundary by the filter, whereas agents trained with it mostly stop on their own before it; both effects replicated on unused scenarios. Three pre-registered training interventions (a published correction penalty, a Beta policy, a reward without a risk term) removed most self-stops, but filter-blocked stalls replaced them, and total deadlock changed by at most about 5 points. A recovery layer that leaves the filter unchanged and plans multi-step manoeuvres at stalls with a shortest-path cost reduced total deadlock from 53--59\,\% to below 1\,\% and raised success by 39--47 points in a pre-registered test, without collisions; about 14\,\% of episodes still timed out while moving. The result held on new scenario sets, with position noise and with a sensor-built map, but a wrong map restored about 20\,\% deadlock, and the planner alone, given the exact map, was more successful (95\,\% against 85--86\,\%). All results are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} safe reinforcement learning; control barrier functions; safety filter; deadlock; recovery planning; mobile robot navigation.

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================
Safety filters are a common way to make learned controllers usable in safety-critical settings~\cite{hsu2024safetyfilter,brunke2022safe}. The learned policy proposes an action, and a filter, often a control barrier function (CBF) quadratic program~\cite{ames2017cbfqp,ames2019cbf}, replaces it with the closest action that keeps the system in a safe set. In robot navigation, the safe set excludes a margin around every obstacle, and the filter guarantees under its modelling assumptions that the robot never enters that margin.

The guarantee says nothing about reaching the goal. For model-based nominal controllers it is known that CBF filters can create undesired stable equilibria on the boundary of the safe set~\cite{reis2021undesired,tan2024undesired}, and multi-robot CBF controllers can deadlock~\cite{grover2023deadlock,jankovic2024liveness,chandra2025deadlock}. When the nominal controller is a reinforcement-learning (RL) policy, the situation is less clear. The policy may have been trained with the filter in the loop~\cite{pizarro2025filtering,yang2026cbfrl,krasowski2023provably}, it reacts to observations rather than to the true state, and its behaviour near obstacles is learned rather than designed. Recent work shows that RL under a perfect, least-restrictive filter loses no optimality~\cite{oh2026optimal}, and that adding a penalty on the filter's correction improves the performance of filter-trained agents in trajectory tracking~\cite{pizarro2025filtering}.

That safety mechanisms can impede task progress has been observed before. Yang et al.\ note that a policy unaware of the CBF can behave conservatively~\cite{yang2026cbfrl}. Thumm et al.\ note that fail-safe interventions can cause abrupt braking or aggressive steering~\cite{thumm2023reducing}. Banerjee et al.\ note that shields with fixed backup policies often hinder task progress~\cite{banerjee2024dmps}. In crowd navigation, a planner may decide that all forward paths are unsafe and freeze the robot, although a feasible path typically exists~\cite{trautman2010unfreezing}. Our targeted search did not find a study that, for a single robot driven by a learned policy behind a CBF filter, (i)~separates stalls caused by the filter from stops commanded by the policy, (ii)~checks every failure against a geometric feasibility oracle, and (iii)~relates the dominant mechanism to how the policy was trained. This is the gap we address. The search was targeted, not systematic, so the claim is limited to the work we examined.

This paper studies that question in a deliberately simple and fully reproducible setting. The robot is a unicycle, the obstacles are static circles, and the filter is a one-step discrete-time CBF on the speed~\cite{agrawal2017discrete}. The setting is simple enough that the filter's behaviour can be analysed exactly, and that every timeout can be checked against a geometric feasibility oracle.

The diagnosis leads to a remedy question. If the stalls occur in scenarios that are solvable, a layer that supplies the missing manoeuvre at a stall, with the filter unchanged, should remove them. We test such a layer in two pre-registered rounds, and we compare it with the same planner used without the learned policy, because a planner that needs the map may not need the policy at all.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item[RQ1] Which properties of a one-step discrete-time CBF speed filter determine where and how a filtered robot can stall?
  \item[RQ2] By which mechanisms do learned policies deadlock behind such a filter, and does training with the filter change the mechanism?
  \item[RQ3] Which simple remedies (stochastic deployment, the published correction penalty, heading-level modification, changes to the policy class or reward) remove which mechanism?
  \item[RQ4] Does a recovery layer that plans multi-step manoeuvres at stalls, with the filter unchanged, remove the deadlock, what information and computation does it need, and how does it compare with planning without the learned policy?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Properties of the one-step speed filter} for the unicycle (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). They follow directly from the forward-Euler model and are not new theory, but they determine which remedies can work and give testable predictions: one-step safety is independent of the commanded turn rate (Lemma~\ref{prop:heading}); the headings that permit motion collapse as the barrier approaches zero (Proposition~\ref{prop:cone}, Corollary~\ref{cor:stall}); and at steps where the policy commands a near-zero speed, no test-time speed limit can make the robot move (Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}).
  \item \textbf{A diagnostic procedure} for deadlocks in filtered navigation. It combines a geometric feasibility oracle, a stall taxonomy (filter-blocked vs.\ self-stop) and uncertainty estimates over both training seeds and test scenarios (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The oracle and the thresholds are specific to static circular obstacles and a unicycle.
  \item \textbf{Evidence on two stall mechanisms} from ten PPO agents and three classical controllers (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The mechanisms depend on the training setup, replicate on unused scenarios and on a corrected scenario generator, and respond differently to remedies. Pre-registered training experiments, including a null primary result for the published correction penalty, show that removing self-stops moves the stalls to the filter boundary rather than removing the deadlock.
  \item \textbf{Evidence on planning-based recovery and its limits} (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:recovery} and \ref{sec:r4}). With the filter unchanged, supplying multi-step manoeuvres at stalls removed almost all deadlock in two pre-registered rounds, without collisions. Ablations and baselines show what this requires: a global shortest-path cost, a correct map, and planner calls whose 99th-percentile latency on a laptop was 25\,ms (not a real-time guarantee); and they show that the planner without the learned policy performed better still.
  \item \textbf{An open, pre-registered evaluation}: protocols and analysis code committed before each confirmatory run, per-episode results, committed checkpoints and independent audits of the earlier rounds (Section~\ref{sec:avail}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new safety filter or a new training algorithm. The safety proof of the filter is standard~\cite{agrawal2017discrete} and is restated only for completeness. The correction penalty is the established method of Pizarro Bejarano et al.~\cite{pizarro2025filtering}, and similar penalties on blocked or corrected actions appear in earlier work~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding,saunders2018trial,krasowski2023provably}. We implement only its correction-penalty term, $\alpha\lVert u-u^{\mathrm{exec}}\rVert^2$ with $\alpha=1$. Here $u$ is the policy's sample and $u^{\mathrm{exec}}$ is the executed action after the action bounds and our one-step filter. Our filter replaces their predictive safety filter, and we do not use their safe resets. We use this term as a baseline. What is new is the question it is used to answer: does it remove either stall mechanism in navigation, and which one (RQ3)? Likewise, the recovery layer of Section~\ref{sec:method} combines standard ingredients and is not claimed as a new planner. We use it as an intervention that tests whether the stalls can be escaped with the filter unchanged, and to measure what remains.

\section{Related work}
%=====================================================================
\paragraph{Safety filters and CBFs.} CBF quadratic programs~\cite{ames2017cbfqp,ames2019cbf} and their discrete-time form~\cite{agrawal2017discrete,zeng2021mpcdcbf} are the standard tools for enforcing state constraints around arbitrary nominal controllers. High-order CBFs handle constraints with higher relative degree~\cite{xiao2022hocbf}. Predictive and reachability-based filters offer less conservative alternatives at higher computational cost~\cite{wabersich2021psf,hsu2024safetyfilter}. Robust, measurement-robust and stochastic variants address model and state uncertainty~\cite{kolathaya2019issf,cosner2021mrcbf,cosner2023stochastic,das2026robust}.

\paragraph{Undesired equilibria and deadlock.} Reis et al.~\cite{reis2021undesired} showed that CBF-QPs can introduce asymptotically stable equilibria on the safe-set boundary, and Tan and Dimarogonas~\cite{tan2024undesired} characterised them in general. In multi-robot navigation, deadlock was analysed as a force equilibrium on the boundary~\cite{grover2021deadlock,grover2023deadlock} and resolved through instability of the deadlock set~\cite{jankovic2024liveness} or with discrete-time CBFs~\cite{chandra2025deadlock}. All of these analyses assume a model-based nominal controller. Potential fields have the analogous problem of local minima~\cite{koren1991potential}. In crowd navigation, the ``freezing robot problem'' describes a planner that decides all forward paths are unsafe, so that the robot freezes, although a feasible path typically exists~\cite{trautman2010unfreezing}. The self-stops in Section~\ref{sec:results} resemble this behaviour, but they come from a learned policy behind a filter, not from a planner. Reach-avoid RL gives safety and liveness guarantees by learning a reach-avoid value function~\cite{hsu2021reachavoid}, a different design from post-hoc filtering.

\paragraph{Safety filters and shields in RL.} Shields replace unsafe actions during learning~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding,carr2023shielding}, and CBF filters have been combined with RL in several ways~\cite{cheng2019endtoend,emam2025robust,xiao2023barriernet,pham2018optlayer}. Krasowski et al.~\cite{krasowski2023provably} compare action replacement, projection and masking, and report that a penalty on interventions helps. Thumm et al.~\cite{thumm2023reducing} modify the agent's action proactively to reduce fail-safe interventions, which can otherwise cause abrupt braking or aggressive steering. Banerjee et al.~\cite{banerjee2024dmps} replace the fixed backup policy of model predictive shielding, which they note often hinders task progress, with a local planner that selects recovery actions using the learned policy's value estimates. Pizarro Bejarano et al.~\cite{pizarro2025filtering} study filtering during training, a correction penalty and safe resets for PPO on quadrotor trajectory tracking; they report chattering, but their task has no obstacles and they do not analyse stalls. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2026cbfrl} filter rollouts and add a CBF reward term so that the policy can be deployed without a filter. The term penalises violations of the CBF condition by the policy's action, and the distance between the policy's action and the filtered action. Their navigation agent is a single integrator, which has no heading, so the heading geometry of Section~\ref{sec:theory} does not arise. They report success rates, but they do not analyse timeouts or stalls. Oh et al.~\cite{oh2026optimal} prove that RL under a perfect, least-restrictive filter, one that intervenes only when the nominal action would leave the maximal controlled-invariant set, preserves optimality. CBF-based safe RL for navigation includes~\cite{song2022lidar,marzari2025probabilistic,xie2025certificated,guo2025radar}. Barriers synthesised online from range data remove the known-map assumption~\cite{keyumarsi2024lidar,long2021barrier}.

\paragraph{Policy gradients under clipped or projected actions.} When an action is clipped or projected before execution, many sampled actions lead to the same executed action, and therefore to the same return. Fujita and Maeda~\cite{fujita2018capg} study clipping to action bounds. They propose an estimator that uses the clipping and prove that it is unbiased and has lower variance than the conventional estimator, which ignores the bounds. Chou et al.~\cite{chou2017beta} argue that bounded actions bias the gradient of a Gaussian policy, and propose a Beta policy. In action-constrained RL, projection-based methods can suffer from a zero-gradient problem caused by the coupling between the policy gradient and the projection~\cite{lin2021zerogradient}. Gros et al.~\cite{gros2020projection} find projection onto a safe set generally disruptive for Q-learning, and give corrections that keep policy gradients unbiased. None of these works studies navigation stalls. We use them to frame, not to establish, a hypothesis about self-stops (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Recovery and hybrid planning.} Classical navigation stacks detect a lack of progress and then run recovery behaviours, such as rotating in place or clearing the costmap~\cite{marder2010office,macenski2020marathon2}. Scoring short-horizon motion samples by a global cost-to-go, as our recovery layer does, follows the global dynamic window approach~\cite{brock1999globaldwa}; a cost-to-go from a shortest-path search over the free space is a discrete navigation function, which has no local minima other than the goal~\cite{rimon1992navigation}. Hybrid methods combine RL with planning, for example by using an RL policy as the local planner between the nodes of a roadmap~\cite{faust2018prmrl}. In safe RL, recovery policies~\cite{thananjeyan2021recovery} and model predictive shielding~\cite{bastani2021mps,banerjee2024dmps} take over when the learned policy approaches unsafe states. Their purpose is safety; the recovery layer studied here acts only on liveness and leaves safety to the unchanged filter.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} RL results vary strongly between seeds~\cite{henderson2018matters}, and few-run studies need interval estimates rather than point estimates~\cite{agarwal2021precipice}. Navigation benchmarks such as BARN~\cite{perille2020barn} and Arena-Rosnav~\cite{kastner2023arena}, and community guidelines~\cite{francis2025principles}, stress classical baselines and standardised metrics.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}
%=====================================================================
\paragraph{Robot and environment.} The robot state is $x_t=(p_t,\theta_t)$ with position $p_t\in[0,W]^2$, $W=10$\,m, and heading $\theta_t$. The command is $u_t=(v_t,\omega_t)\in\mathcal U=[0,v_{\max}]\times[-\omega_{\max},\omega_{\max}]$ with $v_{\max}=1$\,m/s and $\omega_{\max}=1$\,rad/s. The simulator integrates
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dyn}
  p_{t+1}=p_t+v_t\,\Delta t\, e(\theta_t),\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\omega_t\Delta t,\qquad e(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^\top,
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t=0.1$\,s. Obstacles are static discs with centres $c_i$ and radii $R_i\in[0.3,0.8]$\,m; the robot is a disc of radius $r=0.3$\,m.

\paragraph{Barriers and filter.} For a safety margin $d$ (0.3\,m) and a wall margin $m$ (0.1\,m), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:barrier}
\begin{aligned}
  h_i(p)&=\lVert p-c_i\rVert-(R_i+r+d) &&\text{(obstacle $i$)},\\
  h_{W}(p)&\in\{p_x-(r+m),\;W-p_x-(r+m),\;p_y-(r+m),\;W-p_y-(r+m)\} &&\text{(walls)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The safe set is $\mathcal S=\{p: h_k(p)\ge 0\ \forall k\}$. The closing rate per unit speed is $a_i(p,\theta)=e(\theta)^\top(c_i-p)/\lVert c_i-p\rVert$ for an obstacle, and $-\cos\theta,\cos\theta,-\sin\theta,\sin\theta$ for the four walls. With $\gamma=0.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (so $\gamma\Delta t=0.05\le 1$), the filter returns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:filter}
  F(x,u)=\big(\min\{\operatorname{clip}(v),\bar v(x)\},\ \operatorname{clip}(\omega)\big),\qquad
  \bar v(x)=\min\Big\{v_{\max},\ \min_{k:\,a_k>0}\frac{\gamma\,h_k(p)}{a_k(p,\theta)}\Big\}.
\end{equation}
Because every constraint bounds the single variable $v$, \eqref{eq:filter} is the exact solution of $\min_{v}(v-v^{\mathrm{nom}})^2$ subject to $a_k v\le\gamma h_k$ and $0\le v\le v_{\max}$.

\paragraph{Policies.} A policy maps an observation $o_t$ to a nominal command. The observation contains the noisy normalised position, $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$, goal distance and bearing, a proximity feature, and 16 range readings up to 3\,m. The closed loop is $x_{t+1}=f(x_t,F(x_t,\pi(o_t)))$. An episode succeeds when the goal is within 0.5\,m; it ends with a collision if the robot body touches an obstacle or leaves the workspace; otherwise it times out after 200/300/400 steps (beginner/intermediate/expert).

\begin{definition}[Stall classes]\label{def:stall}
A timeout is a \emph{stall} if the net displacement over its last $K=100$ steps is below $\delta=0.10$\,m. A stall is
(i) \emph{filter-blocked} (Type~I) if the filter reduced the commanded speed in at least half of those steps;
(ii) a \emph{self-stop} (Type~II) if, in at least half of them, the policy itself commanded a speed of at most $10^{-3}$\,m/s;
(iii) \emph{other} otherwise. A timeout that is not a stall is \emph{moving}.
\end{definition}

\section{Analysis of the one-step speed filter}\label{sec:theory}
%=====================================================================
The statements in this section follow directly from the model \eqref{eq:dyn}--\eqref{eq:filter}, and none of them requires new theory. We state them because they rule out some remedies and give the predictions that Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

\begin{lemma}[Forward invariance~\cite{agrawal2017discrete}]\label{lem:inv}
If $p_0\in\mathcal S$ and $u_t=F(x_t,\cdot)$ for all $t$, then $h_k(p_{t+1})\ge(1-\gamma\Delta t)h_k(p_t)$ for every barrier $k$. In particular, $h_k(p_t)>0$ for all $t$ if $h_k(p_0)>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By convexity of the norm, $\lVert p_{t+1}-c_i\rVert\ge\lVert p_t-c_i\rVert-\Delta t\,v_t\,a_i(p_t,\theta_t)$. If $a_i>0$, the filter enforces $v_t a_i\le\gamma h_i(p_t)$; if $a_i\le 0$, the barrier cannot decrease. Wall barriers are affine in $p$, so the same bound holds with equality. Strict positivity follows from $\gamma\Delta t<1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Turn-rate independence]\label{prop:heading}
Let $\psi:\mathcal X\times\mathcal U\to[-\omega_{\max},\omega_{\max}]$ be any map, and let $F_\psi(x,u)=(\min\{\operatorname{clip}(v),\bar v(x)\},\psi(x,u))$. Then Lemma~\ref{lem:inv} holds for $F_\psi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:dyn}, $p_{t+1}$ depends only on $(p_t,\theta_t,v_t)$, and $\bar v(x_t)$ depends only on $(p_t,\theta_t)$. The turn rate $\omega_t$ affects only $\theta_{t+1}$, which the filter evaluates again at step $t+1$. The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:inv} therefore applies at every step, whatever $\psi$ returns.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Lemma~\ref{prop:heading} is the discrete-time counterpart of a known structural fact. In continuous time, the position barrier has relative degree two with respect to $\omega$, which is the situation high-order CBFs address~\cite{xiao2022hocbf}. It has two practical consequences here. First, the one-step filter can never \emph{steer} the robot away from an obstacle: only the policy, or a heading modification $\psi$, can do so. Second, heading-level remedies for deadlock, such as a tangential turn when the speed limit is active, inherit the safety guarantee without further proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Collapse of the admissible headings]\label{prop:cone}
Fix $p$ with $0\le h_i(p)\le\varepsilon$ for an obstacle $i$, and a speed $\underline v>0$. Every heading $\theta$ with $\bar v(p,\theta)\ge\underline v$ satisfies $a_i(p,\theta)\le\gamma\varepsilon/\underline v$; that is, the angle between $e(\theta)$ and the direction to $c_i$ is at least $\arccos(\min\{1,\gamma\varepsilon/\underline v\})$. As $\varepsilon\to0$, this set of headings approaches the closed half-circle $\{\theta: a_i(p,\theta)\le0\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $a_i(p,\theta)>0$, then $\bar v\le\gamma h_i/a_i\le\gamma\varepsilon/a_i$, and $\bar v\ge\underline v$ requires $a_i\le\gamma\varepsilon/\underline v$. If $a_i\le0$, the inequality holds trivially.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Stall bound]\label{cor:stall}
If, over $K$ consecutive steps, $h_i(p_t)\le\varepsilon$ and $a_i(p_t,\theta_t)\ge\bar a>0$, then the robot moves at most $K\Delta t\,\gamma\varepsilon/\bar a$, whatever speed the policy commands. For a head-on approach ($a_i\equiv1$) at the speed limit, $h_i(p_{t+1})=(1-\gamma\Delta t)\,h_i(p_t)$ holds with equality, so the barrier decays geometrically and the robot approaches, but never reaches, the boundary.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The displacement per step is $v_t\Delta t\le\bar v\Delta t\le\gamma\varepsilon\Delta t/\bar a$. For straight motion toward the centre, the convexity bound in Lemma~\ref{lem:inv} is tight, giving the stated equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Test-time speed limits at near-zero speed commands]\label{prop:typeII}
Let $F'$ be any filter whose executed speed satisfies $0\le v^{\mathrm{exec}}\le\operatorname{clip}(v^{\mathrm{nom}})$, and let $\underline v\ge0$. At every step where the deterministic policy commands $\pi_v(o)\le\underline v$, the executed speed under $F'$ is at most $\underline v$, as it is without a filter; if $\pi_v(o)\le0$, it is exactly $0$. In particular, at the steps of Definition~\ref{def:stall} where the policy commands at most $\underline v=10^{-3}$\,m/s, no test-time speed limit can make the robot move faster than it would without a filter. At these steps, only a different command from the policy can produce motion.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$0\le v^{\mathrm{exec}}\le\operatorname{clip}(\pi_v(o))\le\max\{0,\underline v\}=\underline v$, and $\operatorname{clip}(\pi_v(o))=0$ if $\pi_v(o)\le0$.
\end{proof}
Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII} concerns only the steps at which the policy commands a near-zero speed. It says nothing about:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the other steps of a stall, at which the policy may command a larger speed;
  \item how a different speed limit at earlier steps would change the trajectory, and with it the later observations;
  \item the effect of the filter during training.
\end{itemize}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cone} and Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII} give testable predictions. Filter-blocked stalls should occur at $h\approx0$ with headings within $90^\circ$ of the obstacle direction. Self-stops should occur at $h>0$. At their stopping steps, the speed should be set by the policy's command, not by the test-time speed limit.

\section{A planning-based recovery layer}\label{sec:method}
%=====================================================================
The remedies of Section~\ref{sec:results} change the policy or its training. The recovery layer instead leaves the trained agent and the filter unchanged and sits between them. It only \emph{proposes} commands, and the filter $F$ of \eqref{eq:filter} checks every one, so Lemmas~\ref{lem:inv} and~\ref{prop:heading} hold whatever the layer proposes.

\paragraph{Cost-to-go.} $J(p)$ is the length of the shortest path from $p$ to the goal region through the safe set $\mathcal S$. It is computed once per episode by 8-connected Dijkstra on a 5\,cm grid, the grid of the feasibility oracle, from the obstacle map that the filter uses; positions in blocked cells take the value of the nearest free cell plus the distance to it. On the grid, $J$ is a navigation function~\cite{rimon1992navigation}. It is global information. The ablation replaces $J$ by the straight-line distance to the goal.

\paragraph{Manoeuvres and prediction.} The library contains 65 open-loop sequences of $H=40$ steps (4\,s): turn in place at $\omega=\pm1$\,rad/s for $k\in\{0,4,8,12,16,24,31\}$ steps (31 steps turn the robot by about $\pi$), then drive at $v=1$\,m/s with $\omega\in\{0,\pm0.5,\pm1\}$\,rad/s. Each sequence is rolled out through the exact model \eqref{eq:dyn} \emph{with} the filter \eqref{eq:filter}, so the prediction is what would be executed: the filter can only slow a manoeuvre down. A sequence is scored by $J$ at its end point; sequences that reach the goal rank first, earlier arrival first, and ties go to the lower index, so the planner is deterministic.

\paragraph{Recovery layer C (primary).} When the net displacement over the last 30 steps (3\,s) is below 0.15\,m, the layer takes over (Figure~\ref{fig:alg}). It executes the best sequence, replans every 5 steps (0.5\,s), and hands control back to the policy when $J$ has fallen by 1\,m since the activation started, when no sequence is predicted to gain at least 0.1\,m, or after 100 steps (10\,s). All values were fixed before the experiments and not tuned (Supplementary Table~S2).

\paragraph{Comparison methods.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A (immediate trigger).} Whenever the filter cuts the speed limit below 0.1\,m/s or the policy commands at most 0.05\,m/s, the planner takes over for 1\,s if its best sequence is predicted to end at least 0.5\,m closer, in $J$, than holding the policy's command.
  \item \textbf{B (behaviour, no map).} The trigger of C, then a turn to the tangent of the nearest barrier and 3\,s of tangent following.
  \item \textbf{C with a straight-line cost} (ablation of the global information).
  \item \textbf{Planner only} (Section~\ref{sec:r4}): the planner of C replanned every 0.5\,s from the first step, without the learned policy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Computation.} A planner call rolls out 65 sequences of 40 steps with the filter. A reimplementation that skips only work whose result is known in advance (rows turning in place, rows already at the goal, a second barrier evaluation) produces bit-identical plans at about 0.58 of the CPU time (Section~\ref{sec:r4}).

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
%=====================================================================
\paragraph{Agents.} PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with separate $256\times256$ tanh actor and critic networks, a diagonal Gaussian policy whose samples are clipped by the environment, generalised advantage estimation and bootstrapping at time-limit truncations. Training ran for $10^6$ steps over a three-stage curriculum (beginner, intermediate, expert; advance at 70\,\% rolling success). Five seeds (0--4) were trained without the filter and five with the filter in the loop. In the filtered runs, the buffer stores the policy's own sample, as in~\cite{pizarro2025filtering}. The checkpoint with the highest success on 60 validation episodes (20 per stage), on seeds disjoint from the test seeds, was kept. One hyperparameter set was used for both variants and was not tuned for the filtered setting. All ten runs reached the expert stage within 43k--76k steps.

\paragraph{Baselines.} A go-to-goal heading controller, which ignores obstacles, and a hand-tuned artificial potential field on the range readings~\cite{koren1991potential}. A DWA-style local planner~\cite{fox1997dwa} uses the same observation (16 range readings, goal distance and bearing; no map). It scores 66 constant-command rollouts of 1.5\,s by progress, heading, clearance and speed, and rejects those whose predicted clearance falls below a margin. Without inertia, the dynamic window is the whole action box. Its weights were selected from 12 configurations on 100 validation scenarios (seeds from $1.3\cdot10^6$) by a rule fixed in advance, and then frozen. PPO with the correction penalty of~\cite{pizarro2025filtering} is evaluated as a remedy (Section~\ref{sec:remedies}). PPO-Lagrangian~\cite{stooke2020pid} and MPC with a DT-CBF~\cite{zeng2021mpcdcbf} were not evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Test scenarios.} 100 seeded scenarios per stage (seed bases $2\cdot10^6+10^5 s$). The expert stage requests 8--15 obstacles; obstacles that do not fit are skipped, and 8--12 were placed in these scenarios. A grid oracle (5\,cm, 8-connected Dijkstra on the free space inflated by $r+d$) found a margin-keeping path in all 300 scenarios. Two properties of the generator must be kept in mind:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The goal is clipped to $[1.5,8.5]^2$, so 53 of the 100 expert goals are closer than the configured minimum of 5\,m (maximum 8.9\,m).
  \item Three scenarios (two beginner, one expert) start inside the goal region and count as successes at step 1 for every controller.
\end{itemize}
The 0.3\,m margin and the sampled-action evaluation were chosen after inspecting these test scenarios, so the test set is not a clean held-out set for those two choices.

\paragraph{Fresh scenarios and pre-registration.} To address this, we evaluated the agents on 500 expert scenarios that had never been used (seeds $9\cdot10^6$ to $9\cdot10^6+499$). The hypotheses, decision rules and analysis were committed to the repository before this evaluation was run. The scenarios come from the same generator, so the goal-clipping caveat still applies.

\paragraph{Corrected generator.} A second pre-registered evaluation (E3) removes the clipping. The generator redraws start, heading and goal together until the goal lies inside the workspace at the requested distance. The start stays in the original square, so only the goal-distance artefact changes. It uses 1{,}000 new scenarios per stage (intermediate: seeds from $7.5\cdot10^6$; expert: from $7.6\cdot10^6$), with mean and sampled actions. An exploratory dense variant uses 500 expert scenarios with 15--25 requested obstacles (seeds from $6\cdot10^6$). In the corrected sets, expert start--goal distances range from 5.0 to 9.0\,m (median 5.8\,m).

\paragraph{Heading escape.} As a test-time intervention, we add to the filter a turn-rate modification $\psi$. When the filter reduces the commanded speed, the turn rate becomes $\operatorname{clip}(\omega^{\mathrm{nom}}+\kappa\,s\,\omega_{\max})$. Here $s\in\{-1,+1\}$ is the turn direction that increases the angle between the heading and the direction of the binding barrier (towards its tangent); if that angle is zero, $s$ points to the goal side. By Lemma~\ref{prop:heading}, the safety guarantee holds for every $\kappa$. The value $\kappa=0.5$ was selected from $\{0.5, 1.0\}$ by success on 100 validation scenarios (seeds $1.2\cdot10^6$ onward) using a rule fixed in advance; it was then frozen. The agents are not retrained.

\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.} To test whether self-stops are an artefact of selecting early checkpoints, we also evaluated the final ($10^6$-step) checkpoints of the five filter-trained runs on the fresh scenarios. These checkpoints are local training outputs and are not yet in the public repository; their SHA-256 hashes are recorded.

\paragraph{Conditions and classification.} Each PPO agent was evaluated with the filter off and on, using the policy mean. Each was additionally evaluated with the filter on and actions sampled from the policy, seeded per episode. Every timeout with the filter on was replayed deterministically from its seed and classified by Definition~\ref{def:stall}. This classification is a post-hoc analysis made after the benchmark had been run.

\paragraph{Statistics.} For PPO, the unit of replication is a training seed. Seed-level 95\,\% $t$-intervals over five seeds describe variation between training runs on the fixed scenario set. Comparisons between conditions use a paired hierarchical bootstrap (10{,}000 replicates). Scenarios are resampled jointly for both conditions, because all conditions share the same scenarios, and seeds are resampled within each condition. Proportions for the deterministic baselines use Wilson intervals over episodes. A two-way decomposition of episode success into seed, scenario and residual sums of squares quantifies the two sources of uncertainty. No multiplicity correction was applied, because the contrasts are reported as estimates with intervals and are not used as a family of hypothesis tests. With five seeds, the bootstrap underestimates seed variance, so the intervals should be read as approximate.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The repository contains the committed checkpoints, and regenerating the benchmark from them reproduces the per-episode files byte for byte. All analysis scripts read only stored outputs; Section~\ref{sec:avail} lists them.

\subsection{Two stall mechanisms}
Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} and Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} classify every timeout with the filter on.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PPO trained without the filter.} Filter-blocked stalls dominate: 141 of 175 expert timeouts and 68 of 81 intermediate timeouts, with mean actions. In these stalls the policy commands full speed (median 1.0\,m/s) while the turn rate changes sign a median of 70 times in 100 steps.
  \item \textbf{PPO trained with the filter.} Self-stops dominate: 122 of 195 expert timeouts and 68 of 100 intermediate timeouts. The policy commands zero speed at a median barrier value of 0.41\,m (interquartile range 0.16--0.72\,m), well before the keep-out boundary. In 89 of the 122 expert self-stops, the goal lies within $45^\circ$ of the direction of the nearest obstacle (median $26^\circ$), and the robot barely turns (median $|\omega|$ 0.04\,rad/s).
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} The two classical controllers bracket the PPO behaviour. Go-to-goal, which always commands forward speed toward the goal, fails only by filter-blocking (54 of 54 expert timeouts). The potential field never stalls against the filter: it oscillates in local minima (22 ``moving'' timeouts) or stops (6).
\end{itemize}
The taxonomy is insensitive to its thresholds. Varying the displacement threshold (0.05, 0.10 and 0.20\,m) and the share threshold (30, 50 and 70\,\%) changes the expert counts by at most 3 (filter-blocked) and 3 (self-stop) for either agent type. Varying the window length $K$ over 50, 100, 150 and 200 steps, with the displacement threshold scaled to the window, leaves the counts of the agent trained without the filter unchanged (141 and 34). For the agent trained with the filter, the filter-blocked count ranges over 63--69 and the self-stop count over 119--122.

\subsection{The geometry of filter-blocked stalls matches the analysis}
Proposition~\ref{prop:cone} predicts that a robot blocked at the boundary heads less than $90^\circ$ from the obstacle direction. The data confirm this (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}a).
\begin{itemize}
  \item 617 of the 618 filter-blocked stalls of all PPO conditions have a heading below $90^\circ$; the remaining one is at $91.3^\circ$. The median is $66.8^\circ$, so the robots are typically not facing the obstacle head-on.
  \item The obstacle barrier at the end of these stalls is at most 4.2\,mm, consistent with the geometric decay in Corollary~\ref{cor:stall}.
  \item In all 618 stalls, the active barrier is an obstacle: no wall barrier is below 1\,mm.
\end{itemize}
Self-stops, in contrast, are spread over barrier values from 0 to 1.4\,m (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}b). This is consistent with Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}: at these stops, the speed is set by the policy's command, not by the test-time speed limit. Whether the filter shaped this behaviour during training is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Training with the filter shifts the mechanism; sampling removes self-stops}
Paired comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:paired}) quantify both effects.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Training with the filter.} On the expert stage, it lowered the filter-blocked rate by 14.8 points [5.6, 24.4] and raised the self-stop rate by 17.6 points [8.2, 27.0]. On the intermediate stage, the corresponding changes were $-8.0$ [$-14.8$, $-1.8$] and $+11.0$ [5.6, 17.2].
  \item \textbf{Sampled actions} (agent trained with the filter). The expert self-stop rate fell by 21.8 points [12.4, 31.6], while the filter-blocked rate did not change ($+0.8$ [$-7.6$, 10.0]).
  \item \textbf{Success.} Sampling raised expert success by 15.4 points [7.8, 24.0] for the filter-trained agent, against 5.0 [0.4, 10.4] for the agent trained without the filter.
\end{itemize}
On these scenarios, stochastic deployment acted on the learned mechanism only. The filter-blocked stalls persist, as Proposition~\ref{prop:cone} suggests they should for any policy that keeps a heading within $90^\circ$ of the obstacle. On the corrected generator with longer goal distances, sampling also \emph{added} filter-blocked stalls (Section~\ref{sec:e3}).

In a decomposition of expert success, the test scenario accounts for 25--86\,\% of the sum of squares and the training seed for under 2\,\%. Seed-only intervals such as those in Table~\ref{tab:main} therefore understate the uncertainty about the scenario distribution (Supplementary Section~S7).

\subsection{Corrected generator: honest goal distances}\label{sec:e3}
Table~\ref{tab:e3} and Figure~\ref{fig:e3} summarise the pre-registered E3 evaluation (46{,}000 episodes, plus 6{,}500 in the dense variant; all with the filter on, no collisions, minimum clearance 0.30\,m).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Success drops sharply.} With goals at their requested distance, expert success with mean actions is 37.2\,\% (filter-trained) and 43.1\,\% (trained without the filter). The two sets differ in scenarios as well as in generator, so this comparison is descriptive. A large part of the expert success on the original generator nevertheless comes from goals that clipping moved closer.

\subsection{A classical planner stalls behind the filter too}\label{sec:dwa}
The DWA-style planner shows that filter-blocked stalls are not specific to learned policies (Figure~\ref{fig:dwa}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Without the filter}, it solved 96\,\% [90.2, 98.4] of the 100 expert test scenarios, with no collisions. Its minimum clearance was only 0.027\,m, far inside the filter's 0.30\,m margin.
  \item \textbf{With the filter}, its expert success fell to 48\,\% [38.5, 57.7], and \emph{every} timeout was filter-blocked. The same held on the 500 fresh scenarios (50.0\,\%; 250 of 250 timeouts) and on the corrected generator (15.3\,\%; 847 of 847). All 12 tuning configurations reached 37--39\,\% validation success, so the weights hardly mattered. The planner steers into the band the filter forbids and is held there.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory follow-up.} This check was added after these results and committed before it was run. Raising the planner's own admissibility margin to 0.35\,m, just above the filter margin, removed \emph{all} filter-blocked stalls in all three sets. Success rose to 63\,\% [53.2, 71.8] (test), 67.8\,\% [63.6, 71.7] (fresh) and 43.6\,\% [40.6, 46.7] (corrected generator). The remaining timeouts were stops in which the planner found no admissible rollout and turned in place. A margin of 0.45\,m gave fewer successes and more such stops.
\end{itemize}
For a classical planner, filter-blocking is therefore a mismatch between the planner's margin and the filter's, which a filter-aware design avoids. What remains is the planner's own local-minimum problem. This mirrors the learned agents: training with the filter removes most filter-blocked stalls, and self-stops take their place.

\subsection{Remedies}\label{sec:remedies}
\paragraph{Heading escape (test time, frozen agents).} With $\kappa=0.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:fresh}), the filter-blocked rate of the agents trained without the filter fell by 7.4 points [4.8, 10.2], and their success rose by 7.2 points [5.0, 9.7]. The reduction held in every seed. Self-stops of the filter-trained agents did not change ($+1.5$ [$-0.8$, $3.9$]), nor did their success ($+0.1$ [$-2.2$, $2.4$]). This is expected. The escape is triggered only when the filter reduces the commanded speed, which never happens while the policy commands zero speed (Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}). A heading change triggered during a stop could in principle change the policy's observation and release it, but we did not test this. There were no collisions, as Lemma~\ref{prop:heading} guarantees. The effect is real but small. It is below the 10-point threshold we fixed in advance, and 523 of the remaining 611 timeouts of these agents are still filter-blocked. A one-step heading rule is therefore not enough to resolve Type~I stalls; it is a lower bound on what filter-level heading changes can do.

\paragraph{Correction penalty (E1a, pre-registered).} We trained PPO with the correction-penalty term of~\cite{pizarro2025filtering} ($\alpha=1$, our one-step filter, no safe resets) and an identical control: five seeds and 300k steps per arm, filter in the loop. The primary endpoints were fixed before training: the deadlock rate (filter-blocked + self-stop + other stalls) and success, both with the policy mean on 500 previously unused expert scenarios (seeds from $5\cdot10^6$), with a Holm adjustment over the two tests. Penalty minus control:
\begin{itemize}
  \item deadlock rate: $-5.1$ points [$-11.6$, 1.7] (Holm-adjusted $p=0.28$);
  \item success: $+0.5$ points [$-5.8$, 6.3] ($p=0.87$);
  \item collisions: none in any of the 24{,}000 evaluation episodes.
\end{itemize}
By the pre-registered rule (a reduction of at least 10 points with an interval excluding 0), the penalty does not resolve deadlock in this setting at this budget. The secondary contrasts were not adjusted for multiplicity. With mean actions, they show what changed (Figure~\ref{fig:e1a}): the self-stop rate fell by 19.7 points [6.9, 32.2] and the filter-blocked rate rose by 14.0 points [2.4, 26.6]. In two seeds, self-stops almost vanished (0.0\,\% and 4.4\,\%), but filter-blocked stalls rose to 40\,\% and 33\,\%. The penalty thus appears to trade one mechanism for the other rather than remove deadlock. With five seeds and a large spread between them, this is suggestive, not established. With sampled actions, the arms did not differ. The penalty agents were 5.0 points [0.8, 9.1] less successful than the committed agents, which were trained for $10^6$ steps. Only $\alpha=1$ was tested.

\paragraph{Removing either candidate cause of self-stops (E4, pre-registered).} We trained two further arms, identical to the E1a control except for one change each (five seeds, 300k steps): a Beta policy~\cite{chou2017beta}, whose samples always lie inside the action bounds, so no speed sample is clipped to zero; and a reward without the risk penalty. The primary endpoint was the policy-side stall rate (self-stops plus other stalls in which the filter rarely limited the speed), with the policy mean on 500 previously unused expert scenarios (seeds from $6.5\cdot10^6$). The criterion was a reduction of at least 10 points with a Holm-adjusted interval excluding 0. The control's policy-side stall rate was 33.8\,\% (seed SD 9.3).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Beta policy:} 0.0\,\% in every seed, a difference of $-33.8$ points [$-41.3$, $-25.2$].
  \item \textbf{No risk penalty:} 2.2\,\%, a difference of $-31.6$ points [$-39.4$, $-22.9$] (both Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$).
\end{itemize}
Both criteria hold, so each change on its own is sufficient to remove the self-stops. Neither of the two candidate explanations is \emph{the} cause. Neither change removed the deadlock, however (Figure~\ref{fig:e4}). The filter-blocked rate rose by 33.6 points [27.3, 40.1] (Beta) and 26.2 points [21.0, 31.4] (no risk penalty). The Beta agents' stalls end exactly at the boundary (median barrier 0.000\,m) with a forward speed command (median 0.52\,m/s), so they are not hidden stops. Total deadlock was 39.7\,\% (control), 39.4\,\% (Beta) and 34.2\,\% (no risk penalty). Success did not change for the Beta policy ($-0.2$ [$-6.3$, 5.5]) and rose by 6.2 points [1.2, 11.2] without the risk penalty. There were no collisions in 15{,}000 episodes. As stated in advance, the Beta policy also changes exploration, the risk-free reward also removes an incentive to keep clearance, and neither arm was tuned.

\subsection{Planning-level recovery removes most deadlock}\label{sec:recovery}
This experiment leaves the agents and the filter unchanged and adds the recovery layer of Section~\ref{sec:method}. The protocol, the analysis and all parameter values were committed before the runs; a 2{,}000-episode pilot on separate scenarios checked only technical validity.

\paragraph{Methods.} The recovery layer C and the comparison methods A, B and C with a straight-line cost are defined in Section~\ref{sec:method} (Figure~\ref{fig:alg}).

\paragraph{Design.} The ten committed agents ran on 500 previously unused expert scenarios from the corrected generator (seeds from $8.5\cdot10^6$) with the policy mean, giving 25{,}000 episodes over five methods. The primary comparison is C against the baseline, separately for agents trained with and without the filter. The endpoints are total deadlock (filter-blocked, self-stop and other stalls together) and success, Holm-adjusted over the four tests. The pre-registered claim needed a deadlock reduction of at least 10 points, a rise in success, intervals excluding zero, and no collision.

\paragraph{Primary result.} The claim rule was met for both agent types (Table~\ref{tab:recovery}, Figure~\ref{fig:r3}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trained with the filter:} deadlock fell by 58.2 points [52.4, 64.3], and success rose by 46.7 points [40.6, 52.6].
  \item \textbf{Trained without the filter:} deadlock fell by 53.0 points [48.5, 57.5], and success rose by 38.8 points [34.4, 43.4].
\end{itemize}
All four Holm-adjusted $p$-values are below 0.001, and the difference has the same sign in every seed. There were no collisions in the 25{,}000 episodes, and the minimum clearance was 0.30\,m. The filter intervention rate rose by 13.0 points [6.7, 18.2] for the filter-trained agents and fell by 9.2 points [7.7, 10.5] for the others.

\paragraph{What the claim does not cover.} Deadlock did not all turn into success. Under C, 13.5\,\% and 14.5\,\% of episodes ended as timeouts in which the robot was still moving, against 2.0\,\% and 0.3\,\% for the baseline. Of these 700 episodes, 686 were deadlocks under the baseline. The robot was freed but did not finish within the 400 steps; the median remaining path fell from 8.6\,m to 4.5\,m. C turned only 2 of the 2{,}139 baseline successes into failures. The 23 remaining deadlocks were tight places where no manoeuvre of the library gained 0.1\,m.

\paragraph{Which ingredient matters (secondary, unadjusted).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Global information.} With the straight-line cost, the same trigger and planner left 25.3\,\% and 27.0\,\% deadlock: 24.8 points [21.3, 28.3] and 26.6 points [22.8, 30.4] more than C.
  \item \textbf{Planning.} The fixed tangent behaviour (B) left 9.8\,\% and 22.9\,\% deadlock, and 20.6\,\% moving timeouts for the filter-trained agents.
  \item \textbf{Trigger.} The immediate trigger (A) left more deadlock than C (7.9\,\% and 2.4\,\%) but fewer moving timeouts. For agents trained without the filter it was 9.0 points [6.3, 11.7] more successful than C. It also took control in 84--88\,\% of episodes and often re-activated at the same place.
\end{itemize}
C was the pre-registered primary method, so A's higher success is an exploratory finding.

\paragraph{Cost.} The mean planner call took 13--18\,ms, and the mean controller time was 1--5\,ms per 100\,ms control step, on one laptop core at low priority. The pre-registered limit on the 99th percentile of planner calls (100\,ms) was not met on the conservative measure we recorded: the longest call per episode had a median of 18\,ms and a 99th percentile of 189\,ms. These wall-clock times were measured under background load and are not a real-time guarantee. A later controlled measurement showed that the 189\,ms reflected the measure and the load rather than the planner (Section~\ref{sec:r4}).

\paragraph{Where stalls occur (exploratory).} We replayed 6{,}000 earlier episodes and compared the final state of 2{,}937 stalls with 1{,}585 close passages in successful episodes. The hypotheses were fixed before the run.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The goal was reachable through the safe set from practically every stall position.
  \item In 58.5\,\% [54.2, 62.5] of stalls, the shortest safe path started more than $45^\circ$ away from the goal direction, against 10.9\,\% [8.8, 13.2] of close passages. This held for both stall types (63\,\% and 50\,\%).
  \item The two types otherwise differ. Filter-blocked stalls sit on the boundary, and 26.5\,\% of them are local minima of the straight-line distance. Self-stops occur a median 0.45\,m before it, and in 98.6\,\% of them a straight move towards the goal was still admissible.
  \item A single 4\,s manoeuvre from the stall state gained 1\,m of path length in only 46.5\,\% of stalls. Our prediction of at least 80\,\% failed.
\end{itemize}
Escape therefore typically needs a detour and more than one manoeuvre. This is consistent with the recovery results: receding-horizon planning with global cost information worked, and a fixed behaviour or a local cost did not.

\subsection{Latency, robustness and baselines of the recovery layer}\label{sec:r4}
A second pre-registered round re-measured the planner's latency, tested C under changed scenarios and imperfect information, and compared it with planners that do not use the learned policy. The protocol, the acceptance criteria and the analysis were committed before the runs. A pilot on separate scenarios led to one dated amendment, made before the confirmatory runs, which added an exploratory baseline variant and a supplementary metric; no method, endpoint or test was changed. All scenario sets are new (seeds from $4.2\cdot10^6$), and all ten committed agents use the policy mean.

\paragraph{Latency.} The reimplementation of the planner (Section~\ref{sec:method}) still rolls every candidate through the exact filter model, and the filter still checks every executed action. The two planners produced identical plans in 4{,}000 of 4{,}000 recorded calls and identical episodes in 2{,}000 of 2{,}000 paired episodes, with differences in success and deadlock of exactly zero. Per call, the new planner used 0.58 [0.57, 0.59] of the CPU time. In a dedicated run (4{,}000 episodes, normal process priority, mains power, the user's other applications running at about 33\,\% CPU load), the 99th percentile of the planner call latency was 25.0\,ms, against 45.7\,ms for the original, and 12 of 419{,}777 control steps (0.003\,\%) took 100\,ms or more. Every call above 100\,ms had used less than half of its wall time on the CPU, so it was pre-empted by the operating system; the longest CPU time of a call was 40.6\,ms. The original planner also met the 100\,ms target under this configuration, so the 189\,ms of Section~\ref{sec:recovery} measured the per-episode maximum under background load, not a slow planner. These are measurements on one laptop with a general-purpose operating system, not a real-time guarantee. The full latency distribution and the profiling results are in Supplementary Section~S4.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================
\paragraph{Two different problems.} Most discussions of filter-induced deadlock treat it as one problem of the filter~\cite{reis2021undesired,tan2024undesired}. Our data show two different problems, and which one dominates depends on the training setup. A policy that never saw the filter keeps pushing at the boundary. It is held there because, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cone}, no heading within $90^\circ$ of the obstacle admits motion. This is the learned-policy counterpart of the undesired boundary equilibria of CBF-QPs. A policy trained with the filter is blocked less often, but it mostly stops on its own, typically when an obstacle lies between it and the goal. By Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}, at the steps where it commands a near-zero speed, no test-time speed limit can make it move. Whether the filter contributed to this behaviour during training is a separate question, discussed next.

\paragraph{Why do filter-trained policies stop?} We separate what is observed from what is hypothesised.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Observed.} Self-stops occur at a positive barrier value (median 0.41\,m), mostly when the goal lies roughly in the direction of the nearest obstacle (89 of 122 expert self-stops within $45^\circ$). They are much less frequent in agents trained without the filter: 34 vs.\ 122 of 500 expert episodes on the original scenarios, and 81 vs.\ 577 of 2{,}500 on the fresh scenarios. Sampled actions remove most of them. A heading escape that is triggered by speed reductions leaves them unchanged. They are more frequent at the final training checkpoints than at the selected ones.
  \item \textbf{Not established by these observations.} The cause. None of them is a causal test. The training interventions of E4, reported below, are.
\end{itemize}
We consider two candidate explanations. The first is \emph{action aliasing} in the filtered environment. Every sampled speed $v\le0$ is executed as $0$, and every speed above the limit is executed as the limit. Within these sets the executed action, and therefore the return, does not depend on the sampled value, so the return carries no information about which of these samples to prefer. Related effects of clipping and projection on policy gradients are documented: variance from ignoring action bounds~\cite{fujita2018capg}, bias of Gaussian policies under bounded actions~\cite{chou2017beta}, zero gradients under projection layers~\cite{lin2021zerogradient}, and the need for corrections when projecting onto a safe set~\cite{gros2020projection}. Our setting is closest to clipping after sampling, as studied by Fujita and Maeda, because the filter acts in the environment and the buffer stores the policy's own sample. Those analyses concern gradient variance and bias, not navigation behaviour, so they make aliasing plausible but do not show that it causes self-stops. Under this explanation, the mean could drift into the stopping region near obstacles, where forward commands were often truncated during training, and sampling would occasionally leave it. Both points are consistent with the observations, but not specific to this explanation. The second explanation is the reward: the non-potential-based risk penalty may make stopping near obstacles attractive. The two explanations are not exclusive.
\begin{hypothesis}
Type~II stops arise mainly from aliasing rather than from the reward. Then a policy whose distribution respects the action bounds (for example a Beta policy~\cite{chou2017beta}), or a clipping-aware gradient estimator~\cite{fujita2018capg}, should reduce them, while removing the risk penalty should not.
\end{hypothesis}
E4 tested this hypothesis, and the data reject its second part. A Beta policy removed the self-stops, and so did removing the risk penalty. The diagnostics are consistent with both routes. In the committed agents, every self-stop had a raw speed command at or below zero in at least half of its last 100 steps (median $-0.20$), which clipping maps to exactly zero. In 71\,\% of them, however, the risk term was zero at the stopping position.

The stronger result is what happens instead. Every intervention that removed self-stops replaced them with filter-blocked stalls at the boundary: the Beta policy, the risk-free reward and, partly, the correction penalty. Total deadlock barely changed. The same exchange appears between agents trained without and with the filter, and, in reverse, for a planner that keeps its own margin above the filter's (Section~\ref{sec:dwa}). This suggests a reading that we have not tested directly. Near an obstacle that lies between the robot and the goal, these policies have no way to make progress. Stopping early and being held at the boundary are then two ways of failing, and the training details decide which one appears. If so, removing self-stops is not a remedy. A remedy has to supply the missing manoeuvre around the obstacle, for example by planning or by a multi-step escape. The recovery experiment (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) tested this by intervention. Supplying such manoeuvres at stalls, with the filter unchanged, removed almost all deadlock in both agent types. This supports the reading that the stalls are escapable and that the policies lack the detour. It does not show why they lack it. The geometric analysis also shows that the two stall types are not the same situation: self-stops occur where even a straight move was admissible.

\paragraph{Relation to theory on filtered training.} Oh et al.~\cite{oh2026optimal} prove that RL under a perfect, least-restrictive filter preserves optimality. Such a filter intervenes if and only if the nominal action would make the state leave the maximal controlled-invariant subset of the safety constraint. The filter studied here is not least-restrictive in this sense, under the following assumption and model.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Assumption.} The safety constraint is the margin set $\mathcal S$ that our filter enforces.
  \item \textbf{Controlled-invariant set.} In the model \eqref{eq:dyn}, $v=0$ is admissible and keeps the position fixed. Every state whose position lies in $\mathcal S$ can therefore be kept in $\mathcal S$ indefinitely, so the maximal controlled-invariant set consists of all such states. A least-restrictive filter then intervenes only when the next position would leave $\mathcal S$.
  \item \textbf{Exit condition.} The bound used in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:inv}, $h_k(p_{t+1})\ge h_k(p_t)-\Delta t\,v_t\,a_k(p_t,\theta_t)$ (with equality for walls), shows that leaving $\mathcal S$ through a closing barrier $k$ ($a_k>0$) in one step requires $v_t\,a_k>h_k/\Delta t$. This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. For an oblique approach to an obstacle, the exact speed at which the next position leaves $\mathcal S$ can be larger.
  \item \textbf{Comparison.} Our filter limits the same quantity to $v_t\,a_k\le\gamma h_k$. Both bounds are in m/s, and their ratio is $\gamma\Delta t=0.05$. For a given state, heading and closing barrier, the filter's speed cap $\gamma h_k/a_k$ is therefore at most one twentieth of the speed at which a least-restrictive filter would have to intervene for that barrier. Because the exit condition is necessary and $v\le v_{\max}$, a least-restrictive filter can act on barrier $k$ only where $h_k<a_k v_{\max}\Delta t\le0.1$\,m.
\end{itemize}
If the safety constraint were collision avoidance without the margin, the maximal controlled-invariant set would be larger, and a least-restrictive filter would intervene even less. Oh et al.'s result therefore does not apply directly to our filter, and it does not tell us how much of the gap the filter itself causes. Our reading that much of the remaining gap is a learning problem rests on our own evidence instead:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the oracle finds a margin-keeping path in every test scenario;
  \item most stalls of filter-trained agents are self-stops at a positive barrier value, where the speed is set by the policy's near-zero command rather than by the test-time speed limit (Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}).
\end{itemize}
This does not exclude that the filter shaped the stopping behaviour during training, which is the aliasing explanation above. Whether a less restrictive filter, for example a larger $\gamma$, would reduce the filter-blocked stalls is open.

\paragraph{What the recovery experiments add.} The recovery rounds test the reading above by intervention. Supplying multi-step manoeuvres at stalls removed nearly all deadlock for both agent types, so the stalls were escapable: what the policies lacked was a manoeuvre, not safe space. The ablations locate the missing ingredient in global information. The same planner with a straight-line cost left 25--27\,\% deadlock, and a corrupted map restored about 20\,\%, mostly filter-blocked. The comparison with the planner on its own qualifies the result. Given the exact map at every step, the planner without any learned policy was more successful than policy plus recovery (95.0\,\% against 86.3\,\% and 85.2\,\%), with fewer moving timeouts but slightly more deadlock. In this task, therefore, the learned policy adds no task performance over a planner with the same map, and the recovery layer is a remedy for a given learned policy, not a better navigator. Whether it is useful where the policy encodes behaviour that a planner does not, for example from perception that no map captures, is not tested here. The remaining moving timeouts point to the cost of conservatism: in these episodes the filter lowered the commanded speed in two thirds of the steps, and the robot moved at about a fifth of its top speed.

\paragraph{Implications for remedy design.} Lemma~\ref{prop:heading} means that heading-level remedies come at no cost to the one-step guarantee. The simple escape tested here helps, but only modestly. A heading rule that looks one step ahead cannot plan a way around an obstacle. Multi-step, planning-level recovery removed almost all deadlock (Sections~\ref{sec:recovery} and~\ref{sec:r4}). By Lemma~\ref{prop:typeII}, a remedy acting only on the test-time speed limit, such as a less conservative $\gamma$, cannot produce motion at steps where the policy commands a near-zero speed. It could still change earlier parts of the trajectory, which we did not test. A heading escape triggered only by speed reductions did not reduce self-stops in our evaluation. The correction penalty~\cite{pizarro2025filtering} is an established method. In their formulation the certified action respects the input constraints. In our implementation, $u^{\mathrm{exec}}$ is the action after the action bounds and the filter. The term therefore penalises both the filter's truncation and the box clipping of $v\le0$. It may therefore act on both mechanisms. In E1a it did act on both, but in opposite directions: fewer self-stops and more filter-blocked stalls, with no net reduction in deadlock. This suggests that a remedy for the learned mechanism should be combined with one for the geometric mechanism, for example a heading-level or planning-level escape. The DWA results point the same way. A controller that keeps its own margin above the filter's is never blocked by the filter, but it then needs a way out of its own local minima.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limitations}
%=====================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{External validity.} 2D simulation with exact Euler kinematics, static circular obstacles, exact state for the filter and a known map. Nothing was tested on a robot, and no finite simulation establishes real-world safety. The filter's guarantee holds at the sampled states. Between samples, the chord bound limits the penetration to about 1.4\,mm for the generated obstacle sizes; continuous-time motion is not covered.
  \item \textbf{Scenario design.} On the original generator, goal clipping shortens many goal distances. With honest distances (E3), expert success roughly halves, so success rates on the original sets should not be compared with other benchmarks. Three trivial scenarios add 1--2 points to every success rate in their stages. The layouts are fairly open: the median oracle path is 6--8\,\% longer than the straight line. No feasibility oracle was run on the E3 scenarios.
  \item \textbf{Test-set reuse.} The margin, the sampled-action evaluation and the stall analysis were all developed on the same test scenarios. The pre-registered evaluation on 500 unused scenarios reduces, but does not remove, this threat. Those scenarios come from the same generator, and only the expert stage, mean actions and one value of $\kappa$ were tested. The sampled-action variant of that evaluation and the final checkpoints of the agents trained without the filter were planned but not run.
  \item \textbf{Statistical power.} Five seeds per variant, with 100 scenarios per stage on the original test set, 500 on the fresh sets and 1{,}000 per stage on the corrected generator. With five seeds, the seed-level spread is large (for example, the E1a penalty arm's filter-blocked rate ranged from 6\,\% to 40\,\% across seeds), so effects of a few points cannot be resolved. The intervals are approximate, and there is no multiplicity correction beyond the pre-registered decision rules.
  \item \textbf{Artefacts.} The final checkpoints used for the checkpoint-selection analysis are not yet in the public repository.
  \item \textbf{Construct validity of the taxonomy.} The class thresholds are heuristic, but varying the displacement threshold, the share threshold and the window length changed the expert counts by at most 4.
  \item \textbf{Baselines and tuning.} PPO hyperparameters were not tuned for the filtered setting, and the potential field was tuned by hand. The DWA-style planner was tuned only on 12 configurations; its filter-aware variant was exploratory and chosen after seeing results. PPO-Lagrangian and MPC with a DT-CBF are missing. The correction-penalty arms were trained for 300k steps, not $10^6$, and only $\alpha=1$ was tested.
  \item \textbf{Privileged feature.} The proximity feature in the observation is computed from the true map.
  \item \textbf{Recovery layer.} The planner uses the exact map and state, as the filter does. With local information only, a quarter of the episodes still deadlocked. Its parameters were set in advance and not tuned. About 14\,\% of episodes still timed out, and nothing is proved about reaching the goal. The planner on its own, with the full map, was more successful than C (Section~\ref{sec:r4}), so the experiment shows that the deadlock is removable, not that this architecture is the best one. With a wrong map, about a fifth of the episodes deadlocked again. The sensed map was idealised, and no real perception or mapping stack was tested. The latency was measured on one laptop with a general-purpose operating system; it is not a real-time guarantee. The classical path follower was not tuned beyond one margin.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================
A one-step discrete-time CBF filter made ten learned navigation policies collision-free in simulation, but it did not make them live. Its one-step guarantee does not depend on the commanded turn rate, and at the boundary the headings that admit motion collapse to a half-plane. In this setting, learned policies deadlocked by two mechanisms whose balance depended on training: policies trained without the filter were held at the boundary by the filter, and policies trained with it mostly stopped on their own before it. Stochastic deployment and a heading escape each reduced one mechanism and left the other. Three training interventions, the published correction penalty, a Beta policy and a reward without the risk term, removed most self-stops, but filter-blocked stalls took their place and total deadlock changed by at most about 5 points.

A recovery layer that supplies multi-step manoeuvres scored by a shortest-path cost, with the filter unchanged, removed almost all deadlock in two pre-registered rounds without a collision. It worked because it adds global information: with a straight-line cost a quarter of the episodes still deadlocked, with a wrong map about a fifth did, and the same planner without the learned policy was more successful. About 14\,\% of episodes still ran out of time, mostly because the filtered robot moved slowly. The evidence therefore supports a diagnosis, namely that the policies lack a manoeuvre around obstacles that lie between them and the goal, and it sets a boundary for remedies: what removed the deadlock was information that a planner already has. Whether learned policies can acquire this capability, and how the results transfer to real robots, real perception and moving obstacles, remains open. All results come from simulation, with five seeds per training arm.
